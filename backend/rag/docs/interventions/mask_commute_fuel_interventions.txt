=== SECTION 1: N95 RESPIRATORS vs CLOTH MASKS vs SURGICAL MASKS ===

Citation: Shakya, K.M. et al. (2017). "Evaluating the efficacy of cloth masks in reducing particulate matter exposure." Journal of Exposure Science & Environmental Epidemiology, 27, 352-357. DOI: 10.1038/jes.2016.42
Citation: Langrish, J.P. et al. (2012). "Reducing personal exposure to particulate air pollution improves cardiovascular health in patients with coronary heart disease." Environmental Health Perspectives, 120(3), 367-372. DOI: 10.1289/ehp.1103898
Citation: WHO (2020). "Advice on the use of masks in the context of COVID-19." WHO Technical Guidance.

Filtration efficiency by mask type for PM2.5:
| Mask Type | PM2.5 Filtration Efficiency | Notes |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------|
| N95 (US NIOSH standard) | 95% (≥0.3 µm particles) | Must be properly fitted; no fit = no protection |
| FFP2 (European equivalent) | 94% | Same protection class as N95 |
| N99 | 99% | Higher efficiency; harder to breathe through |
| P100 / FFP3 | >99% | Used in industrial settings |
| Surgical/medical mask | 10–30% (PM2.5) | Designed for droplets, NOT particles |
| 3-layer cloth mask | 15–50% (PM2.5) | Highly variable; material and fit determine efficacy |
| Single-layer cloth mask | <10% (PM2.5) | Essentially ineffective for PM2.5 |
| Scarf / bandana | 5–10% | Minimal protection |

Key findings:
- Shakya et al. (2017): Surgical and cloth masks provide "little protection" against PM2.5; average <30% filtration efficiency
- N95 respirators, when properly fitted with a seal check, reduce PM2.5 inhalation by 85–95% in real-world conditions
- Fit is critical: an N95 worn incorrectly (no seal, beard, wrong size) may perform no better than a surgical mask
- Langrish et al. (2012): Wearing an N95 during air pollution exposure significantly reduced markers of cardiac injury in coronary artery disease patients

India-specific: BIS-certified masks for India:
- N95 equivalent in India: BIS IS 9473:2002 (Class FFP1, FFP2, FFP3)
- FFP2 (IS 9473, Class P2) is equivalent to N95 — 94% minimum filtration
- Many masks sold in India as "N95" may not be BIS-certified — check for IS 9473 marking
- CPCB advisory (2019): "Only N95 and N99 masks are effective against PM2.5 during heavy smog episodes"

Proper N95 usage:
- Perform fit check: cup mask over nose/mouth, inhale sharply — mask should collapse slightly (indicates seal)
- N95s are designed for 8-hour use; replace or discard when damp, soiled, or breathing resistance increases
- Valved N95s filter inhaled air but not exhaled — not recommended for infectious disease but fine for air pollution
- Children: standard adult N95s do not fit properly; child-sized FFP2 masks are available but rare in India

Health considerations:
- N95 increases breathing resistance — not recommended for extended use in COPD or severe respiratory conditions
- If breathlessness develops while wearing N95: remove and rest indoors
- N95 provides no protection against ozone (gas) — only against particulate matter

=== SECTION 2: LPG vs BIOMASS FUEL — HEALTH IMPACT COMPARISON ===

Citation: Balakrishnan, K. et al. (2015). "The burden of disease attributable to household solid fuel use in India: Estimates from a nationally representative sample." Environment International, 85, 262-271. DOI: 10.1016/j.envint.2015.09.015
Citation: WHO (2016). "Burning Opportunity: Clean Household Energy for Health, Sustainable Development, and Wellbeing of Women and Children." WHO Press.
Citation: Pillarisetti, A. et al. (2014). "Patterns of stove usage after introduction of an advanced cookstove: The long-term application of household sensors." Environmental Science & Technology, 48(24), 14525-14533.

Fuel type comparison — indoor PM2.5 concentrations during cooking:
| Fuel Type | Kitchen PM2.5 during cooking (µg/m³) | 24-hr mean PM2.5 |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|------------------|
| Biomass (wood, dung, agricultural residue) | 1000–3000 | 300–800 |
| Charcoal | 500–1500 | 150–400 |
| Coal | 800–2000 | 200–600 |
| Kerosene | 200–600 | 100–250 |
| LPG (liquid petroleum gas) | 50–200 | 20–60 |
| Piped natural gas | 40–150 | 15–50 |
| Biogas (clean, filtered) | 30–100 | 10–30 |
| Electric / induction | 20–50 (non-combustion) | 5–15 |

Health burden of solid fuel use in India (Balakrishnan et al., 2015):
- 300 million+ Indian households use solid fuels for cooking (2015 data; declining with PM Ujjwala Yojana)
- Household solid fuel use causes: 1.1 million deaths per year in India (from IHME GBD 2015)
- Causes: pneumonia, COPD, lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, cataracts, low birth weight
- Women and children most exposed: women cook 3–5 hours/day; children often in kitchen
- Each additional hour of biomass cooking exposure linked to 7% increase in childhood stunting

Transition from biomass to LPG:
- Reduces kitchen PM2.5 by 70–90%
- Reduces 24-hour household PM2.5 by 60–80%
- Associated with 30–50% reduction in acute lower respiratory infections in children under 5
- Women's lung function improves measurably within 1 year of switching to LPG
- India's PM Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY) distributed 90+ million free LPG connections to Below Poverty Line households (2016–2023)

Transition from LPG to electric/induction:
- Further reduces PM2.5 by 40–60% vs LPG
- Eliminates CO and NO2 from combustion entirely
- Requires reliable electricity supply — a constraint in many Indian rural areas
- Electric induction cooking is 85–90% energy efficient (vs 60–70% for LPG)

=== SECTION 3: COMMUTE MODE AND PERSONAL POLLUTION EXPOSURE ===

Citation: Dons, E. et al. (2012). "Impact of time-activity patterns on personal exposure to black carbon." Atmospheric Environment, 55, 271-278. DOI: 10.1016/j.atmosenv.2012.03.030
Citation: Ramos, C.A. et al. (2016). "Exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 during commuting by subway and car in Porto." Atmospheric Environment, 141, 395-404. DOI: 10.1016/j.atmosenv.2016.07.021
Citation: Zuurbier, M. et al. (2011). "Commuters' exposure to particulate matter air pollution is affected by mode of transport." Environmental Health, 10, 10. DOI: 10.1186/1476-069X-10-10

Personal PM2.5 exposure during commuting by mode:
| Commute Mode | Relative PM2.5 Exposure | Key Factors |
|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| Private car (windows open) | High (100%) — reference | Direct exposure to road traffic emissions |
| Private car (windows closed, fresh air mode) | High (~80%) | Cabin filters outdoor air but not fully |
| Private car (windows closed, recirculation mode) | Low–Medium (20–40%) | Best option for car users |
| Motorcycle / two-wheeler | Very High (120–150%) | Rider at road level; no enclosure; directly in exhaust stream |
| Bus (diesel, windows open) | High (90–110%) | Near road-level exhaust of leading vehicles |
| Bus (AC, windows closed) | Medium (50–70%) | Some filtration but older buses have poor filtration |
| Metro / underground subway | Low–Medium (40–70%) | Subway PM2.5 mainly from brake dust (PM10 dominant); less PM2.5 |
| Metro / surface rail | Low (30–50%) | Good enclosure; not in traffic stream |
| Cycling | High–Very High (110–130%) | High breathing rate + road-level position |
| Walking | Medium (60–80%) | Lower breathing rate; can choose routes away from traffic |

Key findings:
- Motorcycle riders have the highest PM2.5 inhalation dose of any commute mode — combining high exposure with high breathing rate from physical exertion
- Car users on recirculation AC: 50–60% lower exposure than on fresh air mode (Zuurbier et al., 2011)
- Cyclists: Higher exposure than car drivers due to breathing rate (3–5x rest) but lower exposure than motorcyclists due to route flexibility
- Metro/subway: Lower PM2.5 from road traffic but higher levels of metal particles from brake/wheel dust — different health profile

Strategies to reduce commute exposure:
1. Use recirculation mode on car AC — single most effective action for drivers (60% reduction)
2. Take metro/bus over motorcycle or open car
3. For cyclists: choose routes with minimal vehicle traffic; wear N95 mask
4. Avoid commuting during peak pollution hours: 7–10 AM, 5–8 PM — shift commute if possible
5. Carpool: reduces road traffic density; reduces per-person exposure by reducing total vehicles
6. Work from home: eliminates commute exposure entirely

=== SECTION 4: PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AND AIR POLLUTION — BALANCING RISKS ===

Citation: Tainio, M. et al. (2016). "Can air pollution negate the health benefits of cycling and walking?" Preventive Medicine, 87, 233-236. DOI: 10.1016/j.ypmed.2016.02.002

The paradox: Exercise increases breathing rate (10x compared to rest), increasing particle intake. But inactivity has its own health risks.

Tainio et al. (2016) breakeven analysis:
- Up to AQI ~100 (PM2.5 ~35 µg/m³): Health benefits of cycling outweigh pollution harms even for daily cycling commutes in most cities
- AQI 100–150 (PM2.5 35–55 µg/m³): Benefits and risks approximately balance; short, low-intensity rides still beneficial
- AQI > 150: Strenuous outdoor exercise risks outweigh benefits; move exercise indoors
- AQI > 200: Even brief outdoor exertion poses health risk for sensitive individuals

Practical recommendations:
- Low-intensity walking (AQI up to 150): generally safe even for sensitive groups if duration < 30 minutes
- Cycling for commute (AQI up to 100): safe with N95 mask
- Running / vigorous exercise: limit to AQI < 100; if AQI 100–150, use N95 and reduce intensity
- AQI > 150: Move all vigorous exercise indoors (gym, home workout)
- Always choose routes with minimal traffic — roadside PM2.5 is 2–3x background
